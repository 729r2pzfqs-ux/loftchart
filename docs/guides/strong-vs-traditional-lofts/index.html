Strong vs Traditional Iron Lofts

What “strong lofts” means, how far loft creep has actually gone, and how to compare sets honestly — with the numbers computed from the spec archive.

Strong lofts are club lofts set 3°–6° lower than the traditional number for that club. Across the sets in this archive confirmed as currently sold, the median blade 7-iron is 34° and the median game improvement 7-iron is 29° — about a club and a half apart under the same stamped number. Neither is wrong; they are different designs for different launch needs. The problems start when sets are compared by number as if the numbers meant the same thing.

The case for strong lofts

Modern distance heads earn their lofts. Thin faces flex and add ball speed; low, deep centres of gravity and wide soles launch the ball several degrees higher from the same loft than a blade can. Give that head a traditional 34° and it balloons — so designers take the extra launch back as loft and sell the difference as carry. For the golfer who hits the ball low, that trade is real help: a 29° 7-iron they can launch beats a 34° one they cannot.

The case against

Three honest costs. First, comparison becomes meaningless: “this 7-iron goes further than that one” says nothing when the lofts differ by five degrees. Second, gapping: a set whose pitching wedge is 42.5° leaves a wide hole to a 54°–56° sand wedge, which is why strong-lofted sets ship with gap wedges and why the gap wedge exists at all. Third, the short irons: strong lofts mean less spin on approach shots, and at some point a 9-iron that flies like an 8-iron stops checking like a 9-iron.

See it in real sets

The cleanest live comparisons are same-brand pairs sold side by side: Mizuno JPX-925 Forged vs Hot Metal (30° vs 28° 7-irons from one catalogue) and Titleist 620 CB vs T100 (35° vs 33°). For the full spread, put any blade next to any game improvement set on their model pages — every chart lists loft, lie and length per club.

How to compare sets honestly

Ignore the numbers on the soles. Line up both loft charts, match clubs by loft, and compare carry and height loft-for-loft on a launch monitor. Then check the wedge end: count the degrees from the set’s pitching wedge to your sand wedge and make sure a wedge ladder of 4°–6° steps fits in between. A set that wins loft-for-loft is genuinely better for you; a set that only wins number-for-number is a relabelling.

Frequently asked questions

What are strong lofts in golf?

Lofts set several degrees lower than the traditional number for that club — a 7-iron at 28 to 31 degrees instead of 34 to 35. Manufacturers pair them with low-CG heads that restore the launch height, so the club flies as high as the old loft did but carries further.

Are strong lofts cheating?

No — but they complicate comparisons. Nothing in the rules defines club numbers, and a strong-lofted head genuinely launches its loft higher than an older design could. The honest criticism is about marketing: a distance gain that comes mostly from loft looks like a technology gain when the club numbers hide it.

Do strong lofted irons actually go further?

Yes, by roughly one club per 3 to 4 degrees — that part is simple geometry. The questions worth asking are whether the ball still flies high enough to hold greens (in a well-designed head, usually yes) and what happens at the wedge end of the set, where a 42 degree pitching wedge leaves a bigger gap to your sand wedge.

Should I play strong or traditional lofts?

Match the design to your launch, not to the distance claim. Slower swings and low-ball hitters benefit from strong-lofted heads because the design adds the height they lack. Faster swings that already launch high lose nothing with traditional lofts and keep tighter wedge gapping and more consistent spin. A launch monitor settles it in ten shots.

Why does my new 7-iron go further than my old one?

Check both loft charts before crediting the face technology. If the new 7-iron is 29 degrees and the old one was 33, you are comparing different clubs with the same label — most of the gain is loft. Every model page on this site lists the chart, and the compare pages put two sets side by side.
